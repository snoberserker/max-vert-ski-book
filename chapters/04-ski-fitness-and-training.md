The most useful preseason workout is the one you can repeat. A heroic weekend of lunges followed by six inactive days is a poor foundation for a winter of repeated efforts. Aim to arrive with a little more capacity and a routine you understand.

This chapter offers general planning ideas for adults. It is not a rehabilitation program or a test of readiness after injury. If a condition, pregnancy, symptoms, or a long period of inactivity affects exercise choices, work with a qualified clinician or coach on an appropriate starting point.

## Build the base before chasing the burn

As a general health baseline, the [CDC’s adult activity guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly, or an equivalent amount of vigorous activity, plus muscle strengthening on at least two days. That is population guidance, not a skiing performance prescription. Some activity is better than none, and your starting point matters.

A practical ski preparation plan needs several kinds of capacity: sustained activity, strength through useful ranges, balance, and the ability to recover between efforts. It also needs time for sleep. Training that repeatedly steals sleep can undermine the winter you are trying to prepare for.

## An adaptable eight-week framework

Think in phases rather than deadlines. Eight weeks is an illustrative planning window, not a minimum that makes skiing safe or a promise of a particular result.

| Phase | Main purpose | A useful question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weeks 1–2 | Establish repeatable activity and familiar movement | Can I repeat this next week? |
| Weeks 3–5 | Gradually build strength and comfortable aerobic time | Am I recovering between sessions? |
| Weeks 6–7 | Practice controlled movement under modest fatigue | Does my movement stay organized? |
| Week 8 | Arrive rested and maintain routine | Am I reducing last-minute overload? |

Use two nonconsecutive strength days, several manageable aerobic opportunities, and recovery time as a starting calendar. Adjust the amount to your existing routine. Increase one demand at a time: frequency, duration, load, or complexity. If you are starting from little activity, your first success may simply be a consistent walk and a coached introduction to strength work.

## Movement families to discuss with a coach

A squat or sit-to-stand pattern helps you practice moving through the legs. A hip hinge asks the hips to do useful work. Step-ups and supported split-stance movements introduce one-leg demands. Calf work and controlled ankle movement deserve attention because a ski stance starts at the foot. Trunk exercises can emphasize maintaining position while the limbs move.

These are movement categories, not mandatory exercises or medical recommendations. A trainer can help select versions, loads, and ranges that match your body and experience. Start with control. External load should follow a movement you can repeat, rather than compensate for one you cannot.

Balance practice can be simple: a stable surface, nearby support, and deliberate weight shifts. A wobble device is not a requirement. Nor does standing on one foot prove that you can manage an unpredictable slope.

## Aerobic work should fit your life

Walking hills, cycling, swimming, or another enjoyable activity can provide repeatable aerobic work. Choose something you can access on a normal weekday. Comfortable conversational efforts are useful for building consistency; harder intervals belong in a plan suited to your conditioning and health.

Do not turn every session into an imitation of an exhausting ski run. Skiing skill is not reducible to thigh discomfort, and fitness work cannot teach snow judgment. You still need appropriate instruction and easy first days.

## Use a weekly example carefully

An illustrative week might contain strength on Monday and Thursday, an easy walk or ride on Tuesday and Friday, a longer comfortable outing on Saturday, and lighter movement or rest on the other days. It is a calendar example, not a required dose. If you ski on Saturday, adjust the week so you do not arrive carrying avoidable fatigue.

Write down how the session felt and how you recovered. Persistent pain, swelling, dizziness, chest symptoms, or an unusual loss of function deserve appropriate medical assessment; they are not a cue to win an argument with the workout.

## Keep training once the lifts open

The season changes the workload. Travel, cold, altitude, and several consecutive ski days can make your normal gym week too much. Maintain helpful habits while adjusting around actual recovery. A short sustainable routine can be more useful than an ambitious plan abandoned in January.

**Field note:** Put your next two realistic activity sessions in the calendar. Specify where, when, and the easy version you will do if time gets tight. Consistency starts with a plan small enough to survive Thursday.
