In Richmond, Vermont, Mickey and Ginny Cochran started running a rope tow in their backyard in 1961. The enterprise eventually acquired the tax status that its neighbors might have guessed at all along. In 1998 Cochran's became a nonprofit. Read the ski area's account of itself and notice the unit in which it describes its ambitions. A child ought to be able to ski. A family ought to be able to come back. [Cochran's Ski Area, its history and mission](https://cochranskiarea.com/mission/)

It is tempting, in telling the history of a little hill, to hurry past the backyard and look for a famous skier to prove the enterprise was worthwhile. Follow a different child for a moment. Suppose she skis there for years, never wins a race, and comes back as an adult with a pair of very small mittens in her pocket. What would a balance sheet have to count in order to register what the tow had done?

A ski area's future is usually drawn as a map of proposed lifts. There is another way to draw it. Start with the people close enough to go on a school night, then ask how many of them could still afford to live there if the place became a success.

## Keep the small places in the picture

A local hill can be a school, a meeting place, and a repeatable winter habit. Its value may be invisible in a destination ranking. It gives people a place to practice after work and a reason to go outside when the weather is ordinary.

If you love a small area, use it when it suits your needs. Read its operating notices. Buy instruction you value. Participate in legitimate club or volunteer opportunities when available. Affection is helpful; practical participation is what keeps a community connected.

Large resorts have their place too. The aim is an ecosystem of ways into skiing, with enough variety that the sport does not depend on one expensive model of a perfect trip.

## Make access a concrete question

Access is more than the lift ticket. It includes transport, clothing, confidence, instruction, physical accessibility, available time, and whether you expect to be treated with respect. Lowering one barrier while ignoring the others can leave a first day unnecessarily hard.

When bringing someone new, explain the ordinary details. Tell them where to meet, what they need, what can be rented, where they can warm up, and how the day can end early. Ask what would make participation easier instead of assuming you know.

Adaptive skiing demonstrates how equipment, instruction, and support can expand possibilities. [Move United](https://moveunitedsport.org/) is a resource for finding adaptive sports programs; confirm the relevant local snowsports offering and capacity directly.

## Treat mountain communities as homes

A resort town exists beyond your weekend. People working in lifts, lessons, food service, maintenance, and patrol need functioning lives there. Consider how your visit interacts with transport, housing, and local services.

Use available transit when practical, follow parking rules, respect closures, and treat staff as people doing skilled work. A difficult weather day is not a personal failure by the person scanning your ticket. Complaints can be specific and civil.

Learn something about the landscape’s longer history, including Indigenous communities and continuing relationships to the place. Use sources connected to those communities rather than treating an acknowledgment as a substitute for learning.

## Keep winter variability in the plan

Snow drought can involve inadequate precipitation, warmth, or both, as [NOAA’s explanation](https://www.drought.gov/what-is-drought/snow-drought) describes. For a skier, the practical consequence is to care about temperature, coverage, and operating terrain as well as total precipitation.

Our editorial view is that resilience has several scales. Individuals can build flexible travel plans and enjoy appropriate local terrain. Operators can explain constraints honestly. Communities and public institutions can make longer decisions about infrastructure, land, water, and climate.

No equipment purchase resolves the whole problem. It is still useful to think carefully about travel, support credible climate work, and ask substantive questions about the places you visit. Avoid mistaking a sustainability slogan for a measured result.

## Let technology serve the day

Forecast tools, digital maps, booking systems, and ski trackers can make planning easier. They can also pull attention away from the people and terrain around you. Use the information that helps the decision, then put the screen away when it competes with awareness.

A vertical total measures vertical. It does not measure the quality of a lesson, the patience you showed a friend, or the pleasure of a quiet run. Keep a little of the day outside the scoreboard.

The [Max Vert snow desk](/data/) and [journal](/blog/) are companions to this book: current starting points for conditions and conversation. A planning tool earns its place when it helps you reach a better real day.


## The ride back up

At Paradise on Mount Rainier, the rope had to come down for summer. At Cochran's the neighbors found a way to keep a tow going across generations. Those two places suggest different answers to the question of what a mountain owes the people who will come after us. Both answers took work. Somebody had to loosen the hardware. Somebody had to turn up the following winter.

If you have a hill you love, you probably know something there that would be hard for a stranger to discover. Where the wind drops on a cold morning. Which bench has room for a parent to help a child wrestle a boot on. Which run brings two friends of quite different abilities to the same chair at about the same time. Give somebody that piece of knowledge. One day they may find themselves on that bench, holding a small foot quite still while its owner tells them an exceedingly long story. The lift will still be there when the story is over.
