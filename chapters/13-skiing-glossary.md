Two inches of what? At breakfast, somebody reading a forecast may mean the snow expected by evening. At a station up the valley, two inches may be the depth on the ground. In a water-supply bulletin those same two inches could mean the water you would get if the whole pack melted. A skier who learns the three nouns attached to that one number can already read a mountain more closely.

Ski language is full of little compressed observations. *Fall line* gives a name to the direction gravity would take a loose ball. *Runout* asks you to keep looking downhill after the interesting part of a slope appears to end. Keep an ear out for the terms people use on the lift. The good ones give you something new to see when you get off.

## Terrain and the mountain

**Alpine skiing:** Downhill skiing, usually with the heel secured in a binding during descent.

**Après-ski:** Social time after skiing. Make transport and any further mountain activity part of your decisions.

**Aspect:** The compass direction a slope faces. Useful when discussing sun, wind, and snow conditions.

**Backcountry:** Terrain outside the managed resort skiing environment. It can require avalanche, navigation, rescue, and mountain travel skills.

**Bowl:** A broad, concave mountain feature. The term describes shape, not difficulty or safety.

**Cat track:** A narrow access road or connecting route that may be used by grooming machinery. It can also be a busy skier connector.

**Chute / couloir:** A narrow, usually steep passage. Local naming varies; the word does not establish suitability.

**Fall line:** The direction most directly downhill at a particular point.

**Glade:** An area of skiing among trees. Tree spacing, snow depth, and hazards vary.

**Groomer:** A trail maintained by grooming machinery, or informally a run on such terrain.

**Inbounds:** Within a ski area’s boundary. It does not mean hazard-free or that every feature is open.

**Moguls / bumps:** Repeated mounds formed through skiing and snow movement, sometimes deliberately shaped.

**Off-piste:** Outside marked or prepared pistes. Management and risk differ by country and mountain; do not equate it automatically with managed resort terrain.

**Piste:** A marked ski run, particularly in European usage.

**Runout:** The lower part beyond a slope or feature; in avalanche discussions, an area where moving snow can travel and accumulate.

**Sidecountry / slackcountry:** Informal terms for easily accessed backcountry, often reached from lifts. The convenience does not remove backcountry hazards.

**Terrain park:** An area with constructed features such as jumps, rails, or boxes. Requires appropriate progression and local park rules.

**Traverse:** Travel across a slope rather than directly down it.

**Tree well:** A depression or loose-snow area near a tree that can present a snow-immersion hazard.

**Vertical / vert:** Elevation descended or gained. A lift’s vertical drop and a skier’s accumulated daily vertical are different measures.

## Snow and weather

**Base depth:** A reported snow depth at a particular location and time. It does not describe every part of a mountain.

**Bulletproof:** Slang for very firm snow. An informal description, not a standardized report.

**Corduroy:** The ridged texture left by grooming machinery.

**Corn snow:** Rounded spring snow grains associated with melt-freeze processes; pleasant skiing depends on timing and conditions.

**Crud:** Informal language for irregular, disturbed snow, often after fresh snow has been skied or changed by weather.

**Crust:** A firmer surface layer over softer snow. Its strength and skiability vary.

**Flat light:** Low visual contrast that makes terrain shape harder to see.

**Freeze-thaw:** Temperature cycling that can change a snow surface between frozen and softened states.

**Powder:** Loose new snow. Depth, density, wind, and underlying coverage still matter.

**Snow level:** The approximate elevation where precipitation changes between rain and snow under forecast conditions.

**Snowpack:** Accumulated snow, with layers and properties that change through time.

**Storm total:** Snowfall accumulated during a defined weather event at a reporting site. Check the measurement period.

**Wind loading:** Snow deposited by wind. Relevant to both surface conditions and avalanche problems; interpretation requires appropriate expertise.

## Equipment

**Binding:** The system connecting boot and ski. Compatibility, setup, inspection, and release testing require qualified service.

**Boot sole length / BSL:** The boot shell’s sole length, typically marked in millimeters. It is different from a footwear size.

**Camber:** An arched part of a ski’s profile, generally lifting the middle above a flat surface when unweighted.

**DIN setting:** Common shorthand for a binding release-indicator value. It is not a setting to copy from another skier or choose from this book.

**Edge:** The metal strip along a ski’s sides that contributes to grip and control.

**Flex:** How equipment bends. Boot flex numbers are not a universal comparison across all brands and models.

**Mondo / Mondopoint:** A foot-length-based sizing system commonly used for ski boots. A proper fit involves more than the number.

**Quiver:** A collection of skis intended for different uses. One appropriate pair can be enough.

**Rocker:** An upward-curving part of the ski profile, commonly toward the tip, tail, or both.

**Sidecut:** The curved outline created when a ski is narrower at the waist than toward its ends.

**Skins:** Removable traction material used on touring skis for uphill travel.

**Waist width:** Ski width underfoot, generally expressed in millimeters.

## Technique, access, and learning

**Carving:** A turn in which the ski edge guides an arc with relatively little sideways slipping.

**Demo:** Trying equipment, often through a shop program; terms and fees vary.

**Nordic skiing:** A family including cross-country and ski jumping; recreational usage often means cross-country skiing.

**Parallel:** A description of the skis’ relationship, often used for a stage or style of turning. It does not establish overall ability.

**Pole plant:** A purposeful pole touch associated with timing and movement in some skiing situations.

**Season pass:** An access product with specified dates, mountains, and conditions. Read the current terms.

**Skier visit:** An attendance measure. Multiple visits may come from the same participant; it is different from a unique-skier count.

**Ski touring:** Traveling on skis using equipment and techniques for ascent as well as descent. Touring can involve backcountry hazards.

**Telemark:** A free-heel skiing discipline associated with a distinctive staggered turning stance.

**Wedge:** A position with ski tips closer together than tails, commonly used in early instruction. Learn its use with an instructor.

**Whiteout:** Conditions in which visibility and visual reference are severely reduced. Local descriptions vary; choose conservative actions.

## Keep learning the language

If someone says a run is “easy,” ask what they mean: wide, groomed, low-angle, uncrowded, or familiar? Those are useful details. “Easy” by itself mostly tells you something about the speaker.

For technique questions, start with an instructor. For binding questions, a qualified technician. For avalanche language, a recognized course and the regional avalanche service. Using the right word is useful; knowing when to ask for help is more useful.
